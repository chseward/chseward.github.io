Personal statement

I am a Staff Genomics Scientist at the Pacific Northwest Research Institute (PNRI). PNRI made my position independent of a single lab in 2025; since July 2025 my effort has been supported by Claudia Carvalho’s grants for long-read rare-disease genomics, and I continue my own projects. My training combines computational biology and bioinformatics with bench work across microbiology, cell biology, neuroscience, imaging, genetics and genomics, plus BSL-2 pathogen work, animal research and protected human clinical data, so I design analyses that fit the biology and the experimental design. After predoctoral work on bacterial pathogenesis with Marcia Goldberg (Harvard Medical School) and Michelle Dziejman (University of Rochester), I spent more than a decade with Lisa Stubbs, from graduate student at the Institute for Genomic Biology (University of Illinois) to staff scientist at PNRI, studying how social challenge, stress and maternal care reshape gene regulation and metabolism in the brain (Saul et al., 2017; Seward et al., 2022). My dissertation identified the WNT effector Tcf7l2 as a link between metabolic regulation, hypothalamic stress responses and social behavior. At PNRI I helped lead the Stubbs lab’s move in 2020, founded the institute’s imaging core and lead its genomics computing. In Claudia Carvalho’s group I developed XCITE, which measures whole-chromosome X-inactivation skew from standard PacBio or Oxford Nanopore genomes, agrees with the clinical HUMARA assay (r = 0.97, n = 12) and, where phasing allows, places structural variants, indels and SNVs on the active or inactive X; in a cohort of more than 80 women, 12 of 16 carrier mothers carried the duplication on the inactive X (Seward et al., 2026, ASHG poster). With the EpiSign team we are also testing whether the MECP2 duplication episignature can be read from long-read methylation (manuscript in preparation). Building on the epigenomics I did with Lisa Stubbs, this work has given me hands-on experience in clinical human genomics: structural variant discovery from long-read, short-read and optical-mapping data, DNA methylation, and rare-disease family studies across the Carvalho lab’s full cohort (about 860 individuals) and about 1,400 GREGoR Consortium genomes, more than 2,000 genomes in all, with collaborators at Baylor College of Medicine. Projects from the past three years include co-investigator work on an NIH R01 on transmissible cancer (spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA-seq) and the Galas Project pilot on maternal stress and pregnancy complications. With Lisa Stubbs, independently of my PNRI appointment and with PNRI’s approval, I am a co-investigator on a proposed genomics study of stress and gestational diabetes in a diverse US pregnancy cohort, which grew out of the Galas pilot; it would pair long-read genomes with blood RNA-seq to build and validate a test that predicts gestational diabetes risk early in pregnancy, and if funded I would be its genomics lead. From 2022 to 2025 I co-managed, with Lisa Stubbs, a team of seven or more technicians, students and postdocs. My goal is to bring genomic regulation, epigenetics and rigorous held-out validation to problems with direct applications to patients.

Contributions to science

  1. Microbial pathogenesis and evolution. Bacterial pathogens subvert host cells with secreted effector proteins, but non-canonical type III secretion system (T3SS) effectors in Vibrio cholerae were poorly understood. With Michelle Dziejman I developed yeast as a model system to define the host-cell interactions and phenotypes of the V. cholerae effector VopX (Seward et al., 2015), work that later showed VopX modulates host actin dynamics (Ulbrich et al., 2025), and helped map how ToxR-like regulators control T3SS-dependent cytotoxicity. I also used comparative genomics to chart the evolution of environmental Sulfolobus acidocaldarius populations and of a multi-drug-resistant ESBL and MCR-1-producing Escherichia coli lineage, my first genomics work. My role: I designed and ran the yeast and molecular experiments, and led or co-led the genome analyses.
  2. Cross-species genomics of social behavior. Whether animals share a molecular program for responding to social challenge was unknown. As a member of the Gene Networks in Neural and Developmental Plasticity group at the Institute for Genomic Biology, I adapted genomic and epigenomic methods to non-traditional social species, contributing to some of the first histone ChIP-seq studies in honey bees and threespined sticklebacks (Bukhari et al., 2017). Combined with our time courses in mice, which showed that social challenge drives coordinated metabolic and neural programs in the brain (Saul et al., 2017), these studies identified an evolutionarily conserved transcriptional regulatory toolkit for social response across species (Saul et al., 2019). I later showed an epigenomic shift in the amygdala that marks the transition to maternal behavior in alloparenting virgin female mice (Seward et al., 2022). My role: I generated chromatin and expression data, contributed analyses across these studies, and led the alloparenting study as co-first author.
  3. Gene regulatory mechanisms in neurodevelopment. Many neurodevelopmental genes act through cell-type-specific isoforms and regulatory elements that are hard to resolve. With collaborators at the Institute for Genomic Biology and PNRI, I led the genomic data analysis and its integration with transcriptomic, chromatin and imaging data. This work showed that loss of the AUTS2 long isoform, brain-wide or in Calbindin-lineage cells, produces a specific suite of brain, behavioral and molecular pathologies (Song et al., 2024); that Galnt17 loss causes developmental delay and social deficits with cerebellar vermis pathology (Chen et al., 2022); and contributed, with Saurabh Sinha’s group, to SEAMoD, an interpretable neural network for cis-regulatory analysis of differentially expressed genes (Bhogale et al., 2023). I also contributed to studies of ZSCAN5 transcription factors at RNA polymerase III genes and of histone H1.4 phosphorylation. My role: genomic data generation and analysis, and integration across data types.
  4. Stress, metabolism and pregnancy complications. TCF7L2 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for type 2 diabetes and is also associated with gestational diabetes, but its role in the brain was unclear. Building on our finding that social challenge drives coordinated metabolic and neural programs (Saul et al., 2017), my dissertation identified Tcf7l2 as a link between metabolic regulation, the stress response of hypothalamic cells and social behavior. Two manuscripts in preparation, on which I am senior author, show that mice lacking the Tcf7l2 long isoform brain-wide or only in the hypothalamus have disturbed circadian rhythms, susceptibility to diet-induced obesity, and abnormalities of the thalamus, third ventricle and hypothalamus. As co-investigator on the Galas Project pilot (PIs Lisa Stubbs and Donna Russell), a study of the genetics of maternal stress and pregnancy complications, I characterized candidate genes such as TCF7L2 in clinical and model-system data. My role: I led the Tcf7l2 studies as senior author and developed the bioinformatics analyses for the pilot.
  5. Long-read genetics and epigenetics of X-linked disease. Skewed X-inactivation can protect a woman who carries a harmful X-linked variant, or leave her affected. The clinical assay (HUMARA) reads one site, fails in many women, and cannot say which X carries the variant. In Claudia Carvalho’s group, with collaborators at Baylor College of Medicine, I developed XCITE, which measures whole-chromosome X-inactivation skew from standard long-read genomes by phasing reads on tandem-repeat length and nearby variants and comparing promoter methylation between the two X chromosomes (Seward et al., 2026, ASHG poster). It agrees with HUMARA (r = 0.97, n = 12), remains informative where HUMARA fails, and, where phasing allows, places structural variants, indels and SNVs on the active or inactive X: in 12 of 16 carrier mothers of MECP2 duplications the duplication is on the inactive X. With the EpiSign team we are also testing whether the MECP2 duplication episignature defined on methylation arrays can be read from long-read methylation data (manuscript in preparation). XCITE is designed to let laboratories read X-inactivation, and which X carries a variant, from genomes they already have, including in women the clinical assay cannot read. My role: I designed and built XCITE and the long-read episignature analyses and lead the two first-author manuscripts in preparation; I contributed structural-variant analyses to a third manuscript, led by J. D. Bengtsson, on the structure of Xq28 duplications.
